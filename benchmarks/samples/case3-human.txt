Zero-fare bus programs attract strong political support because the equity benefit is immediate and easy to explain to voters. Yet five years of municipal audit records from 2019 to 2023 tell a narrower story once operating budgets and commuter habits enter the picture.

Evidence from Tallinn and Kansas City separates two outcomes that transit debates often treat as the same thing. After removing bus fares, both cities saw bus ridership rise by 14% to 19%, with most of those new trips coming from low-income residents and riders who already took the bus regularly. Private car use barely moved. Across household travel surveys, car-trip displacement averaged just 3.2%, largely because habitual drivers pick cars for faster door-to-door travel times, predictable scheduling, and wider route coverage rather than savings on a single bus ticket.

The budget gap is harder to absorb. For a mid-sized metropolitan bus network, giving up farebox revenue creates roughly an $18.6M annual operating subsidy gap just to keep existing service headways intact. Agencies that cover that gap by delaying fleet maintenance or cutting off-peak bus frequency end up hurting reliability, which drives riders away again. Universal free bus fares help poor households, but they rarely get drivers off arterial roads. Means-tested passes paired with dedicated bus lanes cost less to sustain and tackle the speed problem directly.
