Serving long-context language models usually stalls on memory bandwidth before compute ever saturates. During autoregressive decoding, the key-value (KV) cache grows linearly with sequence length and batch size, forcing operators to cap concurrency or spread a modest model across extra GPUs. Quantizing that cache looks like an obvious fix. The catch is that attention weights are brittle: strip away too much precision in the wrong channels, and retrieval accuracy collapses well before perplexity looks alarming.

We tested asymmetric INT4 KV-cache quantization on Llama-3-8B using a single A100-80GB GPU at a 16k context window. Relative to the FP16 baseline, INT4 cuts KV-cache VRAM usage by 38.4% and pushes batch decoding throughput up by 1.72x. Uniform quantization across the entire sequence still hurts needle-in-a-haystack retrieval, though. Keeping two small regions in FP16 (specifically the first four attention-sink tokens and a sliding window of the 128 most recent tokens) brings WikiText-2 perplexity degradation down to 0.41 points without erasing the throughput gain.

Why does uniform scaling break down in the first place? Dettmers et al. (2022) and Hooper et al. (2024) showed that extreme activation outliers concentrate systematically in specific channels tied to attention routing rather than appearing at random. Our measurements across all 32 decoder layers match that pattern. Because outlier magnitudes stay relatively consistent along the channel dimension, channel-wise scaling preserves long-document summarization quality far better than per-tensor calibration, even when the rest of the KV cache runs at 4-bit precision. Dequantization kernels still add minor latency overhead at batch size 1, so the hardware trade-off has not disappeared, yet for batched long-context serving, holding the sink and local window tokens in FP16 makes INT4 KV-cache serving practical in production.
