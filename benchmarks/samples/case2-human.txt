Most college students keep their phone face up next to their laptop while studying, usually convincing themselves a quick check will not hurt. You need it for two-factor logins, group chats about assignments, and calendar alerts anyway. The problem is that push notifications break concentration far more than a three-second glance makes it feel.

The numbers from classroom and lab studies are pretty blunt. In one controlled study of 184 undergraduates, students who received periodic text alerts scored 22% lower on reading-comprehension recall than students whose phones were left in another room. Looking at the task logs explains why. After each alert, it took an average of 4.3 minutes for students to get back into steady reading, so five or six messages during an hour-long chapter quietly eat up nearly half the session.

Even turning notifications off does not fully solve it. Both Rosen et al. (2013) and Ward et al. (2017) found that simply having a silenced phone sitting on the desk pulls away working memory, because part of your attention stays busy ignoring it. What stands out most in these studies is how badly students misjudge their own habits. People report feeling totally fine switching back and forth between messages and notes, yet their recall scores keep dropping right alongside the alert count. Leaving the phone in a backpack or across the room sounds almost too simple to matter, though based on the recovery-lag data it probably does more for retention than any complicated study app.
